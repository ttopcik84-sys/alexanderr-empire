Introduction & Research Question

How was the empire built—and why did it break apart?

Alexander the Great's campaigns created an empire stretching from Greece and Egypt to Central Asia and northwestern India. Yet it did not remain united after his death in 323 BCE. How could such vast territories be conquered so quickly but prove so difficult to hold together?

This project argues that the military and political foundations established by Philip II, together with Alexander's tactical leadership, made conquest possible. However, governing diverse territories and securing an accepted succession proved much harder than winning battles. The kingdoms that emerged after Alexander's death reveal both the limits of his political achievement and the endurance of his Hellenistic legacy.

I. Before the Conquest

359–334 BCE

The Empire Alexander Inherited

Alexander's conquest of Persia is often remembered as the achievement of one extraordinary man. However, his success would have been far less likely without the foundations established by his father, Philip II. As historian Ian Worthington argues, “Without Philip’s achievements for Macedonia there would have been no Alexander the Great” (2014, p. 4). This challenges the idea that Alexander's victories were solely the result of his individual military genius.

When Philip became king in 359 BCE, Macedonia was politically unstable and vulnerable to foreign threats. He transformed the kingdom by reorganizing its army, strengthening the Macedonian phalanx with long spears known as sarissas, and improving coordination between infantry and cavalry. These reforms created a disciplined and mobile army that Alexander would later use against Persia (Worthington, 2014, pp. 33–37).

Philip's victory over Athens, Thebes, and their allies at Chaeronea in 338 BCE also established Macedonian dominance over much of Greece. However, military supremacy did not necessarily mean political unity. Many Greek cities resented Macedonian control, showing that Philip's authority depended heavily on military strength (Worthington, 2014, pp. 81–102).

When Philip was assassinated in 336 BCE, Alexander inherited an experienced army and a powerful kingdom, but also faced immediate threats to his authority. His suppression of resistance, particularly the destruction of Thebes in 335 BCE, demonstrated that maintaining Macedonian power required force as well as military skill (Worthington, 2014, pp. 121–137).

Alexander's greatest initial advantage, therefore, was not a victory he had personally achieved, but the political and military foundations he inherited. His later conquests would demonstrate how effectively he could use those foundations, while also exposing the difficulties of maintaining an empire built through conquest.

II. The Great Conquest

334–330 BCE

Why Persia Fell

In 334 BCE, Alexander crossed into Asia with the army his father had built. Although Macedonia possessed a highly disciplined military force, victory over the Persian Empire was far from guaranteed. Persia controlled vast territories, had considerable resources, and commanded armies capable of challenging Alexander. His success therefore depended not only on the strength of his soldiers, but also on how effectively he used them (Worthington, 2014, pp. 139–141).

The Battle of Issus in 333 BCE demonstrated Alexander's ability to exploit battlefield conditions. The narrow coastal plain limited the Persians' numerical advantage, while Alexander directed his cavalry toward Darius III. When the Persian king fled, his army began to lose cohesion. Philip Freeman challenges the traditional portrayal of Darius as an incompetent opponent, arguing that “Darius was no fool and was a far better general than many ancient historians claim” (2011, p. 115). This suggests that Alexander's victory should be understood through his tactical decisions rather than simply the supposed weakness of his enemy.

Alexander demonstrated similar abilities at the Battle of Gaugamela in 331 BCE. He countered the Persian chariots, drew enemy cavalry away from its position, and exploited an opening in the Persian lines. Worthington emphasizes “Alexander’s cunning and his men’s precision and split-second timing” (2014, p. 192). The battle illustrates how Alexander combined personal leadership with the discipline of the army inherited from Philip II.

Following Gaugamela, Alexander captured Babylon, Susa, and Persepolis, breaking the political power of the Achaemenid Empire, although resistance continued in its eastern territories. However, military victory did not automatically guarantee political stability. Alexander increasingly relied on existing Persian administrative structures and local officials to govern his expanding territories (Worthington, 2014, pp. 193–200).

His conquest demonstrated extraordinary military effectiveness, but it also raised a more difficult question: could an empire won through military strength remain united without its conqueror?

III. Beyond Persia

330–323 BCE

The Limits of Conquest

The defeat of the Persian Empire did not mark the end of Alexander's ambitions. He continued eastward into Bactria, Sogdiana, and eventually India. However, these campaigns revealed a fundamental difference between conquering territory and maintaining control over it.

In Central Asia, Alexander faced persistent resistance from local populations. Unlike the major battles against Darius III, these conflicts often involved smaller, mobile forces that were difficult to defeat decisively. Philip Freeman observes that Alexander “was no closer to a solution of the Sogdian situation” (2011, p. 251), despite years of campaigning. This demonstrates that military superiority did not necessarily translate into lasting political stability.

In 326 BCE, Alexander defeated King Porus at the Battle of the Hydaspes in India. Yet his greatest obstacle soon came from his own soldiers. At the Hyphasis River, the Macedonian army refused to advance farther east. As Worthington explains, “They were exhausted from all the fighting and could not care less about any worldwide empire” (2014, p. 251). Their refusal exposed the growing conflict between Alexander's ambitions and the physical and psychological limits of his army.

Arrian, an ancient Greek historian writing centuries after Alexander's campaigns, describes the soldiers' reaction when their king finally agreed to turn back. According to Arrian, the soldiers celebrated “because by them alone he suffered himself to be conquered” (Arr. Anab. 5.29.1; trans. Chinnock, 1884). This episode demonstrates that even Alexander's authority had limits. The soldiers who made his conquests possible could also prevent him from continuing them.

Alexander also faced difficulties integrating different peoples into his empire. Alexander's attempt to introduce the Persian court ritual of proskynesis among his Macedonian companions created tensions, as many regarded the practice as inappropriate. His increasing reliance on Asian soldiers further contributed to dissatisfaction within the army (Worthington, 2014, pp. 233–234, 277–279).

These developments suggest that Alexander's greatest challenge was no longer defeating foreign armies, but maintaining the loyalty of his soldiers and the stability of his territories. His extraordinary military achievements had created an empire whose political unity remained uncertain.

When Alexander died in Babylon in 323 BCE, these unresolved tensions would become particularly significant as his generals struggled to determine who should inherit his power.

IV. The Fall of the Empire

323–281 BCE

A Divided Legacy

When Alexander died in Babylon in 323 BCE, he left behind an enormous empire but no universally accepted successor. His generals had conquered vast territories together, yet their loyalty was largely directed toward Alexander himself rather than an established political system. Without his leadership, the unity of the empire became increasingly difficult to maintain.

Following Alexander's death, his commanders gathered in Babylon to determine the empire's future. His half-brother Philip III Arrhidaeus and his posthumous son Alexander IV were recognized as kings, but effective authority remained with regents and military commanders (Waterfield, 2011, pp. 19–25). Although the commanders initially sought to preserve the monarchy, competing ambitions soon undermined their agreements. As historian Robin Waterfield explains, their loyalty had restrained their personal ambitions, but “the bond had dissolved on his death” (2011, p. 17). This suggests that Alexander's empire depended heavily on his personal authority rather than institutions capable of ensuring a peaceful succession.

Over the following decades, the generals, known as the Diadochi, fought for control of the empire. The Battle of Ipsus in 301 BCE was particularly significant, as the defeat of Antigonus weakened one of the strongest attempts to reunite Alexander's territories. By 281 BCE, the struggles of the original successors had produced a political world increasingly divided among competing kingdoms (Waterfield, 2011, pp. 140–154, 197–212).

However, the disappearance of a unified empire did not mean that Alexander's achievements were entirely lost. Waterfield argues that “the Successors consolidated the Conqueror’s gains” (2011, p. x). The Ptolemaic kingdom in Egypt, the Seleucid Empire in Asia, and eventually the Antigonid kingdom in Macedonia maintained important elements of Macedonian rule while contributing to the development of Hellenistic culture.

Ultimately, Alexander's achievements reveal the difference between conquering an empire and establishing a lasting political system. His military victories transformed the ancient world, but the state he created could not survive as a unified kingdom without him. Nevertheless, the kingdoms that emerged from its division preserved and extended parts of his legacy for generations.

This book examines Alexander's military campaigns, leadership, and the challenges he faced while expanding his empire.

This book examines the conflicts between Alexander's successors and explains how his empire was divided into separate Hellenistic kingdoms.
